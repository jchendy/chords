Style deep dive 1

Learn Hendrix style: the hands, the shapes, the feels

What Jimi Hendrix actually does on the guitar, taken apart the way a course would take it apart — the thumb over the neck and the chord it opens up, the pentatonic box under that chord and the box above it, the Hendrix chord, the minor pentatonic against a major chord, the wah rocking with the pick — and put back together as eight feels with rhythm, lead and mixed parts in each, every one of them playable here and a link away from the Jam tab. The songs are analysed as reference points; the lines are written from the idiom, not copied. Every claim is cited at the foot of the page.

He tuned a half-step down (E♭ standard) on most of the records, so what is fingered as E sounds as E♭.[6] Everything on this page is written in the fingered key, which is how players talk about it.

1. Where it comes from

Hendrix learned by ear, from records, playing Muddy Waters, B.B. King, Albert King and Freddie King lines off 45s with friends in Seattle.[25] Then came four years as a sideman on the chitlin' circuit — the Isley Brothers (he is the guitar on their 1964 "Testify"), Little Richard's Upsetters, sessions with Don Covay and King Curtis[19] — and a run of dates behind Curtis Mayfield and the Impressions with Billy Cox.[21] That apprenticeship is the rhythm style: Mitch Mitchell said Jimi was "the first person I ever met who could play all of those incredible Curtis Mayfield rhythm parts right off the top of his head",[21] and Cox names "Little Wing", "Castles Made of Sand" and "Drifting" as the Mayfield in him.[7] The other teachers were Steve Cropper — 6ths slid on the third and fifth strings, muted chords on the backbeat, one-note fills — whose devices a university lesson lays out from "Green Onions" and "Soul Man",[29] and the blues players whose bends and vibrato he made his own: Albert King ("Red House" grew out of King's "Travelin' to California"[6]), B.B. King's wrist vibrato,[10] Buddy Guy.

Two academic readings frame the rest. Milton Mermikides (Gresham College, 2025) treats the guitar as a fretboard with many places for one note, and Hendrix as a player who chose the place for its colour; he shows the chord-melody fluency ("two- to three-note elaborations that extend and connect chords"), the sixteenth-note swing of "Little Wing" moving phrase to phrase between even and heavily swung, and the E7♯9 as one of only four "all-interval tetrachords", a tonic that keeps its dissonance instead of resolving it.[1] John Hanford's University of Washington dissertation on Band of Gypsys reads the late music as "a neotraditional work comprising linked traditional and progressive musical features", blues and R&B and jazz drawn on in the licks, the chord usage and the pitch organisation.[2]

2. The hands: the thumb, the shape, the split chord

Almost everything in the rhythm style follows from one grip. Instead of a first-finger barre, the thumb comes over the top of the neck and frets the root on the low E string; the first finger is then free, and so are the others.[12] [6b] With the bass note under the thumb, the hand strikes the D, G and B strings on their own — the triad inside the E shape, the "split chord" — and the spare fingers hammer onto the shape: the 4th onto the 3rd on the G string (a sus4), the 6th onto the 5th on the B string, the 9th onto the octave on the top string.[8] [9] Mermikides notes the choice of exactly those three colours — 4th, 6th, 9th on a major triad; 7th, 9th, 11th on a minor — is not arbitrary: they add colour without a leading-tone pull, "grounded" extensions that keep the chord where it was.[1]

The same hand converts every barre shape: the E-shape major and minor, the A-shape and C-shape majors with the 3rd often in the bass, the minor-7 shapes with the root on the 5th string, the add9.[7b] The Pickup Music course goes shape by shape through the CAGED system and pairs each with the records: the E shape and the thumb with "Little Wing" and "Bold as Love", the A shape and the slides that connect two shapes with "Wait Until Tomorrow" and "Castles Made of Sand", the G shape's double stops with "The Wind Cries Mary", the C shape's major-7 colours with "Castles" and "Angel".[11]

The grips

Most of these are one hand. The thumb-over E shape is the core; the split chord, the hammered 4th, the 6th and the 9th are that hand with one finger moved, so they sit under it as variations, and the 9th chord under the 7♯9 the same way. Every one is in the Chord finder, tagged Hendrix with the name given here. The fingering is in the dots — T is the thumb over the top of the neck — and the number beside a diagram is the fret it starts at for the chord named; a shape with no number sits at the nut, where a ring above a string is an open string and an x a string left out.

The changes, with the grips

The common changes his songs run on, played with these grips: the chords alone — whole and partial, the thumb's bass note and the split chord, the stabs and the 9ths — with no fills or runs, so the hand can learn the changes before the lines between them. Open in drills puts the same changes on the Drills tab with the part's own strumming pattern — the thumb's bass note and the split chord, the stabs — at any tempo, and any other pattern a click away. Neck on any card draws the chord as it's fretted, or the scale under the hand, and lights each note as it sounds — the way the Jam tab's neck follows a part.

The Hendrix chord. E7♯9 is E, G♯, D and G: a dominant 7th with the ♭3 on top as a ♯9 — both thirds at once, a tritone between the 3rd and the 7th, a semitone clash between G and G♯. It was jazz and R&B vocabulary long before 1967 (the Beatles had it on "Taxman"; Hendrix himself first recorded it on the Isleys' "Testify"), and "Purple Haze" is where it became a rock tonic.[5] [20] On "Foxy Lady" he plays a simpler form of the grip without the major 3rd.[16]

3. The scales: where the notes live

The box under the chord. The E-shape barre and the "E shape" pentatonic box share their root on the low E string, so with the thumb chord under the hand, the box is under the hand too: the fills between chords are the pentatonic of the chord you are holding, played off the top three strings of the same position.[9] [8] The Blackstar lesson maps each chord of an A–F♯m–D–E progression to its own major-pentatonic box (shapes 1, 5, 3 and 4) and connects them by descending lines; the app's Jam tab does the same thing in the Chords reading with the position window.

The minor pentatonic over a major chord. The solos on "Purple Haze", "Voodoo Child (Slight Return)" and "Hey Joe" are the E minor pentatonic — E G A B D — over chords that are major or dominant; "using a minor scale over major chords creates that signature tension".[17b] [17] [15] The ♭5 (B♭ in E) turns it into the blues scale, and the major 3rd is let in against it as a passing colour.[15b] On the slow blues both pentatonics are mixed — "the B7 chord voicing, B minor pentatonic, and B major pentatonic" layered in "Red House".[16b] In the app, a part marked blues plays the minor pentatonic over a major chord in the Pentatonic reading and the blues scale in Scales; the parts below say which.

The box at the 12th and the box above it. "Hey Joe" is soloed from the E minor blues scale at the 12th fret;[15] "The Wind Cries Mary" from the F major pentatonic in 12th position;[15c] "All Along the Watchtower" from the C♯ minor blues scale at the 9th with Dorian notes.[15d] Mermikides describes the solo on "Purple Haze" as Dorian over E5, F♯5 and D5;[1] Hooktheory's analysis puts the "Crosstown Traffic" verse in C Dorian.[33] The app writes the Dorian 6th and the 9th as free notes — exact pitches the reading may not otherwise offer.

Moving between shapes. The lead lines connect the boxes rather than staying in one: slides along a string, a phrase up in the next box and a drop back, the same slur-and-slide shape moved along the neck.[10] The unison bend — the B string bent a whole tone up to the note held on the E string — is the sound of "All Along the Watchtower"; the "catch" or exchange bend pushes two strings and sounds them on the way up and down; the vibrato is wrist rotation, B.B. King's, added on top of a bend.[10] In the parts, a note with reach may sit up to five frets past the position, and comes back.

The shapes on the neck

Each drawn in the fingered key of the records it belongs to. The ringed notes are the roots; a dimmed note is in the scale but not in the chord it's played over.

Playing them

The scales as drills, over the band: up the box and down it, the blue note, the step into the box above, the minor scale against the major chord, the Dorian colours, the two pentatonics mixed, the Mixolydian. Neck on a card shows where each note is as it sounds; Open in drills puts the same scale, box and tempo on the Drills tab, where the pattern, the notes a beat and the shapes are yours to change.

10. How this was made

What was done, and what was not. This page and the Hendrix genre in the app were written by Claude (an AI model, working in Claude Code) at the owner's request, in September 2026. The research was reading: the sources in the list below were fetched and read as text — a Gresham College lecture transcript, the abstract and description of a University of Washington dissertation, a London College of Music analysis, a university lesson on Steve Cropper, course pages from Berklee and Ohio State, the Wikipedia articles on the songs and on the 7♯9 chord, magazine and web lessons (Guitar Player, Premier Guitar, MusicRadar, Happy Bluesman, Blackstar, Pickup Music, Jon MacLennan, guitarclub.io, Riff Ninja, Guitar Control, Texas Blues Alley), interviews with Billy Cox, Mitch Mitchell (as quoted) and Eddie Kramer, a 1967 interview with Hendrix, and the legal sources on chord progressions. No recordings were listened to and no videos were watched: the tools used cannot play audio or video, and YouTube transcripts could not be retrieved. Several pages could not be read — Guitar World's lessons and Hooktheory's analyses are behind a subscription wall, the Ultimate Guitar tab pages render only in a browser, the dissertation's full text (51 MB, downloaded) had no extractable text layer — and where a fact came only from a search engine's summary of such a page, the source list says so. The rest is the model's own knowledge of the music, checked against the sources where they overlap.

The parts were then written the way every part in this app is written (docs/STYLES.md): from the idiom — where the weight falls, which strings, which notes, how a phrase ends — never from a transcription, and with no signature riff of his reproduced. Each part is a figure with variants, fills that know whether the next bar changes, lead lines, and a beginner's version, in the interval notation the engine realises into notes on the neck; the engine gained what these parts needed — free (chromatic) notes, a reach past the position, the blues palette over major chords, the 7♯9 and 9th grips, the split chord, unison bends, trills, the wah — and each feature is held by a test. The Chord finder tags the grips named on this page. The tabs on this page are realisations of those parts, rolled from a fixed seed so the page reads the same each time; the sound is the app's own recorded guitar and synthesized band. Tempos are approximate; keys are the fingered keys. Where the page says "as heard in", it means the way of playing on that record, not its notes.
