Style deep dive 1

Learn Hendrix style: the hands, the shapes, the feels

What Jimi Hendrix actually does on the guitar, taken apart the way a course would take it apart — the thumb over the neck and the chord it opens up, the pentatonic box under that chord and the box above it, the Hendrix chord, the minor pentatonic against a major chord, the wah rocking with the pick — and put back together as eight feels with rhythm, lead and mixed parts in each, every one of them playable here and a link away from the Jam tab and the Drills tab. The songs are analyzed as reference points; the lines are written from the idiom, not copied. Every claim is cited at the foot of the page.

He tuned a half-step down (E♭ standard) on most of the records, so what is fingered as E sounds as E♭.[6] Everything on this page is written in the fingered key, which is how players talk about it.

1. Where it comes from

Hendrix learned by ear, from records, playing Muddy Waters, B.B. King, Albert King and Freddie King lines off 45s with friends in Seattle.[25] Then came four years as a sideman on the chitlin' circuit — the Isley Brothers (he is the guitar on their 1964 "Testify"), Little Richard's Upsetters, sessions with Don Covay and King Curtis[19] — and a run of dates behind Curtis Mayfield and the Impressions with Billy Cox.[21] That apprenticeship is the rhythm style: Mitch Mitchell said Jimi was "the first person I ever met who could play all of those incredible Curtis Mayfield rhythm parts right off the top of his head",[21] and Cox names "Little Wing", "Castles Made of Sand" and "Drifting" as the Mayfield in him.[7] The other teachers were Steve Cropper — 6ths slid on the third and fifth strings, muted chords on the backbeat, one-note fills — whose devices a university lesson lays out from "Green Onions" and "Soul Man",[29] and the blues players whose bends and vibrato he made his own: Albert King ("Red House" grew out of King's "Travelin' to California"[6]), B.B. King's wrist vibrato,[10] Buddy Guy.

Two academic readings frame the rest. Milton Mermikides (Gresham College, 2025) treats the guitar as a fretboard with many places for one note, and Hendrix as a player who chose the place for its color; he shows the chord-melody fluency ("two- to three-note elaborations that extend and connect chords"), the sixteenth-note swing of "Little Wing" moving phrase to phrase between even and heavily swung, and the E7♯9 as one of only four "all-interval tetrachords", a tonic that keeps its dissonance instead of resolving it.[1] John Hanford's University of Washington dissertation on Band of Gypsys reads the late music as "a neotraditional work comprising linked traditional and progressive musical features", blues and R&B and jazz drawn on in the licks, the chord usage and the pitch organization.[2]

2. The hands: the thumb, the shape, the split chord

Almost everything in the rhythm style follows from one grip. Instead of a first-finger barre, the thumb comes over the top of the neck and frets the root on the low E string; the first finger is then free, and so are the others.[12] [6b] With the bass note under the thumb, the hand strikes the D, G and B strings on their own — the triad inside the E shape, the "split chord" — and the spare fingers hammer onto the shape: the 4th onto the 3rd on the G string (a sus4), the 6th onto the 5th on the B string, the 9th onto the octave on the top string.[8] [9] Mermikides notes the choice of exactly those three colors — 4th, 6th, 9th on a major triad; 7th, 9th, 11th on a minor — is not arbitrary: they add color without a leading-tone pull, "grounded" extensions that keep the chord where it was.[1]

The same hand converts every barre shape: the E-shape major and minor, the A-shape and C-shape majors with the 3rd often in the bass, the minor-7 shapes with the root on the 5th string, the add9.[7b] The Pickup Music course goes shape by shape through the CAGED system and pairs each with the records: the E shape and the thumb with "Little Wing" and "Bold as Love", the A shape and the slides that connect two shapes with "Wait Until Tomorrow" and "Castles Made of Sand", the G shape's double stops with "The Wind Cries Mary", the C shape's major-7 colors with "Castles" and "Angel".[11]

The grips

Most of these are one hand. The thumb-over E shape is the core; the split chord, the hammered 4th, the 6th and the 9th are that hand with one finger moved, so they sit under it as variations, and the 9th chord under the 7♯9 the same way. Every one is in the Chord finder, tagged Hendrix with the name given here. The fingering is in the dots — T is the thumb over the top of the neck — and the number beside a diagram is the fret it starts at for the chord named; a shape with no number sits at the nut, where a ring above a string is an open string and an x a string left out.

The changes, with the grips

The common changes his songs run on, played with these grips: the chords alone — whole and partial, the thumb's bass note and the split chord, the stabs and the 9ths — with no fills or runs, so the hand can learn the changes before the lines between them. Open in drills puts the same changes on the Drills tab with the part's own strumming pattern — the thumb's bass note and the split chord, the stabs — at any tempo, and any other pattern a click away. Neck on any card draws the chord as it's fretted, or the scale under the hand, and lights each note as it sounds — the way the Jam tab's neck follows a part.

The Hendrix chord. E7♯9 is E, G♯, D and G: a dominant 7th with the ♭3 on top as a ♯9 — both thirds at once, a tritone between the 3rd and the 7th, a semitone clash between G and G♯. It was jazz and R&B vocabulary long before 1967 (the Beatles had it on "Taxman"; Hendrix himself first recorded it on the Isleys' "Testify"), and "Purple Haze" is where it became a rock tonic.[5] [20] On "Foxy Lady" he plays a simpler form of the grip without the major 3rd.[16]

3. The scales: where the notes live

The box under the chord. The E-shape barre and the "E shape" pentatonic box share their root on the low E string, so with the thumb chord under the hand, the box is under the hand too: the fills between chords are the pentatonic of the chord you are holding, played off the top three strings of the same position.[9] [8] The Blackstar lesson maps each chord of an A–F♯m–D–E progression to its own major-pentatonic box (shapes 1, 5, 3 and 4) and connects them by descending lines; the app's Jam tab does the same thing in the Chords reading with the position window.

The minor pentatonic over a major chord. The solos on "Purple Haze", "Voodoo Child (Slight Return)" and "Hey Joe" are the E minor pentatonic — E G A B D — over chords that are major or dominant; "using a minor scale over major chords creates that signature tension".[17b] [17] [15] The ♭5 (B♭ in E) turns it into the blues scale, and the major 3rd is let in against it as a passing color.[15b] On the slow blues both pentatonics are mixed — "the B7 chord voicing, B minor pentatonic, and B major pentatonic" layered in "Red House".[16b] In the app, a part marked blues plays the minor pentatonic over a major chord in the Pentatonic reading and the blues scale in Scales; the parts below say which.

The box at the 12th and the box above it. "Hey Joe" is soloed from the E minor blues scale at the 12th fret;[15] "The Wind Cries Mary" from the F major pentatonic in 12th position;[15c] "All Along the Watchtower" from the C♯ minor blues scale at the 9th with Dorian notes.[15d] Mermikides describes the solo on "Purple Haze" as Dorian over E5, F♯5 and D5;[1] Hooktheory's analysis puts the "Crosstown Traffic" verse in C Dorian.[33] The app writes the Dorian 6th and the 9th as free notes — exact pitches the reading may not otherwise offer.

Moving between shapes. The lead lines connect the boxes rather than staying in one: slides along a string, a phrase up in the next box and a drop back, the same slur-and-slide shape moved along the neck.[10] The unison bend — the B string bent a whole tone up to the note held on the E string — is the sound of "All Along the Watchtower"; the "catch" or exchange bend pushes two strings and sounds them on the way up and down; the vibrato is wrist rotation, B.B. King's, added on top of a bend.[10] In the parts, a note with reach may sit up to five frets past the position, and comes back.

The shapes on the neck

Each drawn in the fingered key of the records it belongs to. The ringed notes are the roots; a dimmed note is in the scale but not in the chord it's played over.

10. For teachers

What each lesson is for, what goes wrong first, and how to tell when it is learned. The lessons are the ones in Start here; each names its cards. Every fingering on this page says whether the sources describe it or it is the usual way (the tag under each grip), and every claim about the records is cited; the playing itself was never heard by the author (see How this was made), so treat the tab as an account of the idiom, not a transcription.

Common faults, and how to hear them

DeviceWhat goes wrongWhat to listen for
The thumb overThe thumb presses so hard the wrist locks, or so lightly the low E buzzes; the A string is muted by the thumb's pad.Play the bass note alone: a clean low E, and the A string still rings when struck.
The split chordThe bass note and the triad land together instead of one, "and"; the high e rings when it should be left.Two distinct strokes a beat apart; only D, G and B in the second.
The hammered colorsThe hammered note is weak because the finger comes down late or from too low; the chord dies under it.The 4th, 6th or 9th as loud as the note it lands on, the other strings still ringing.
The 7♯9The low E and the top e sound, because nothing mutes them; the G and G♯ blur into one.Four notes only — mute the low E with the thumb, the top e with the flesh of the ring finger. The clash should be audible, not muddy.
The 9th grip and the trillThe ring-finger bar leaves the top e dead; the trill is even rather than lazy and swallows the beat.Three strings under the bar, all sounding; the trill inside the triplet, the beat still felt.
BendsUnder pitch by a quarter tone, or the vibrato starts before the bend arrives.Play the target note fretted, then bend to it; the pitch matches before the wrist moves.
The unison bendThe two strings don't meet; the top note is fretted too lightly and dies.The beat frequency slows to nothing as the bend arrives; both notes ring after.
The wah scratchThe foot moves against the pick, or the strings ring instead of scratching.Toe down on the downstrokes, heel on the up; a dead percussive sound with the chord's pitch only where the pattern says.
The walk-upThe line arrives late on the new chord's root, or the chord comes early.The 5th of one chord lands exactly on the beat as the root of the next.
The waltz riffThe triplet feel flattens to straight eighths; the middle of the beat gets filled.Three to the beat felt, the middle empty where the part leaves it.

When it is learned

  • The thumb bass and the split chord steady at the practice tempo for eight bars, then at the record's tempo, on Em, G, Am and Bm without looking.
  • The hammered 4th, 6th and 9th clean on any thumb-over chord, and the 4th on the A shape.
  • Sliding 6ths through a I–IV–V in two keys, the slide landing on the beat.
  • The 7♯9 struck with only its four notes sounding, and the riff under it in time with a bass line.
  • The E minor pentatonic box at the 12th up and down at four notes a beat, then the same box at the nut connected to the box above it.
  • The 4th bent to the 5th in pitch, and a step-and-a-half from the 4th to the 6th, each with vibrato only after arrival.
  • The 9th grip slid in on beat three of a 12/8 bar, the trill inside the triplet.
  • The wah scratch for four bars without the strings ringing, the chord landing on the "and of 2".
  • The walk-up round the cycle of fourths, each 5th becoming the next root, at 82.
  • Any study on this page played through twice at its tempo with the band.

11. How this was made

What was done, and what was not. This page and the Hendrix genre in the app were written by Claude (an AI model, working in Claude Code) at the owner's request, in September 2026. The research was reading: the sources in the list below were fetched and read as text — a Gresham College lecture transcript, the abstract and description of a University of Washington dissertation, a London College of Music analysis, a university lesson on Steve Cropper, course pages from Berklee and Ohio State, the Wikipedia articles on the songs and on the 7♯9 chord, magazine and web lessons (Guitar Player, Premier Guitar, MusicRadar, Happy Bluesman, Blackstar, Pickup Music, Jon MacLennan, guitarclub.io, Riff Ninja, Guitar Control, Texas Blues Alley), interviews with Billy Cox, Mitch Mitchell (as quoted) and Eddie Kramer, a 1967 interview with Hendrix, and the legal sources on chord progressions. No recordings were listened to and no videos were watched: the tools used cannot play audio or video, and YouTube transcripts could not be retrieved. Several pages could not be read — Guitar World's lessons and Hooktheory's analyses are behind a subscription wall, the Ultimate Guitar tab pages render only in a browser, the dissertation's full text (51 MB, downloaded) had no extractable text layer — and where a fact came only from a search engine's summary of such a page, the source list says so. The rest is the model's own knowledge of the music, checked against the sources where they overlap.

The parts were then written the way every part in this app is written (docs/STYLES.md): from the idiom — where the weight falls, which strings, which notes, how a phrase ends — never from a transcription, and with no signature riff of his reproduced. Each part is a figure with variants, fills that know whether the next bar changes, lead lines, and a beginner's version, in the interval notation the engine realizes into notes on the neck; the engine gained what these parts needed — free (chromatic) notes, a reach past the position, the blues palette over major chords, the 7♯9 and 9th grips, the split chord, unison bends, trills, the wah — and each feature is held by a test. The Chord finder tags the grips named on this page. The tabs on this page are realizations of those parts, rolled from a fixed seed so the page reads the same each time; the sound is the app's own recorded guitar and synthesized band. Tempos are approximate; keys are the fingered keys. Where the page says "as heard in", it means the way of playing on that record, not its notes.
